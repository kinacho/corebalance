One of the most popular mantras of index and passive investing is the famous phrase: *“Buy and hold.”* Many investors interpret this literally, assuming that once they select their initial [index funds](/blog/index-funds-vs-etfs-comparison) or ETFs, their only task for the next 20 years is to deposit money and completely forget about their broker's screen.

However, there is one crucial exception to this rule of inactivity: **portfolio rebalancing**.

If you never adjust your positions, the market will do it for you—and likely not in the way you want. In this article, we will look at the actual consequences of failing to rebalance your portfolio, how it impacts your risk level, and what history teaches us about the effects of asset drift.

---

## 1. Understanding Asset Drift

When you design your investment portfolio, you select a combination of equities (stocks) and fixed income (bonds) based on your risk tolerance. For example, a classic balanced portfolio:
* **60% Equities (Stocks)**
* **40% Fixed Income (Bonds)**

Over the long term, equities tend to deliver a significantly higher average annual return than fixed income. Consequently, your stocks will grow at a much faster rate than your bonds.

If you let 5, 8, or 10 years pass without making any adjustments, the weight of your stocks will increase naturally. Your balanced 60/40 portfolio could gradually morph into an **85% stock and 15% bond** split. This phenomenon is known as **asset drift**.

---

## 2. The Three Consequences of Failing to Rebalance

Allowing your portfolio to drift unchecked has serious implications for your financial well-being:

### Consequence A: Your Risk Profile Creeps Out of Control
The problem with your portfolio shifting from 60/40 to 85/15 is not the theoretical return (which will likely be higher during a bull market), but that **you no longer hold the portfolio designed for your risk tolerance**.

If you chose to hold 40% in fixed income, it was because you could not tolerate severe drawdowns or because your investment horizon was not extremely long. By drifting to 85% equities, your portfolio has become highly volatile and risky without you consciously deciding to make it so.

### Consequence B: You Suffer Devastating Losses During Market Crashes
The true danger of asset drift is exposed when a stock market crash hits (such as the dot-com bubble in 2000, the financial crisis of 2008, or the COVID-19 crash in 2020).

Suppose the stock market drops by **40%**:
* **If you had rebalanced** and maintained your 60% stock target, the overall drop in your portfolio would be moderated by your 40% bond cushion, which typically remains stable or even rises during crises.
* **If you did not rebalance** and allowed stocks to climb to 85% of your portfolio, the crash will hit your capital much harder. Under severe market stress, you are far more likely to panic and make the ultimate mistake: selling your funds at the absolute bottom.

### Consequence C: You Miss the Opportunity to "Buy Low, Sell High"
Rebalancing acts as an automatic, disciplined mechanism to sell overvalued assets and buy temporarily undervalued ones.

If you don't rebalance, you forfeit the extra return generated by buying more shares of your equity index funds when they are cheap (for example, right after a market crash) using the proceeds from bonds that held their value.

---

## 3. What Does History Teach Us?

Let's look at how this played out in a real-world scenario: the bear market of the 2008 financial crisis.

Imagine two investors who started with a balanced 60/40 portfolio in 2003:
* **Investor A rebalanced annually:** In 2008, when the subprime crisis hit, their portfolio took a hit, but systematic rebalancing forced them to sell bonds (which were trading high) to buy stocks (which were at multi-year lows). When the market recovered sharply starting in 2009, Investor A's portfolio bounced back with impressive speed.
* **Investor B never rebalanced:** By 2008, their portfolio had drifted naturally to an 80/20 allocation due to the stock gains of the preceding years. When the crash hit, their losses were dramatic. Because they lacked sufficient bond reserves to buy cheap equities, their portfolio took years longer to fully recover.

---

## 4. How to Prevent Drift Without Obsessing

You don't need to rebalance your portfolio every week or month; doing so too frequently will only generate unnecessary transaction costs or tax events (depending on your country's regulations).

Most [Bogleheads](/blog/bogleheads-portfolio-beginners) recommend one of two simple approaches:

1. **Calendar-Based Rebalancing:** Choose one or two dates a year (for example, every January and July) to review your portfolio and make the necessary adjustments.
2. **Drift-Threshold Rebalancing (The 5% Rule):** Check your portfolio periodically. If any major asset class deviates by more than 5% from its target weight (e.g., a fund that should be 30% shifts to 36%), it's time to rebalance.
